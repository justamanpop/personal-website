How to actually finish a project

29th August 2026

Introduction

For me and a lot of programmers, a common pain is starting a ton of projects enthusiastically, working on it for a few days, then getting bored and abandoning it. These are my observations on why I was able to finish this one off.

What I built and why

App blocker. Wanted to waste less time on the Chrome browser on my phone. Chrome for Android does not support browser extensions, so it had to be an app. The existing app blockers in the play store allowed me to edit my block lists anytime, and thus I simply unblocked chrome when I felt an urge to use it, making the blocker useless.

Learnings

1. Build to solve your own problems

Finding a non basic CRUD project to build itself can be a challenge, and when started, we're vague on what we actually want to achieve, leading to aimless work and eventually abandonment, going off to start another new project again.

But if you want to solve a specific issue you have in YOUR everyday life, then it's easier to do it. You have a specific goal or experience in mind, and you can immediately begin using your own creation to get feedback on its usability, driving your changes till you're satisfied.

When you see and actually use your creation to improve your life, motivation is also higher, reducing the likelihood of it being abandoned.

Finally, if it helped you, odds are, other people might also find it useful. But even if you remain the only user of it, that's OK.

2. Break work into small, doable steps

When you have an idea, after the enthusiasm fades off, the large list of things you want in the software is daunting. You feel demotivated and tired just looking at how much work is left, often leading to abandoning it. Breaking into small steps help alleviate this.

For example, when I built the app blocker, my first milestone was to build an app that blocked just the Google Chrome app 24/7. My first small step was just to detect when chrome was opened. Once I figured that out, the next step was to draw an overlay on the screen (a block screen). Then it was making the process run constantly as a background service. And suddenly, milestone reached!

With this, you get periodic bursts of motivation on seeing progress, which is crucial in you not abandoning it.

3. Build basic working thing first, polish later

The first version of the app was just to block Google Chrome all day, nothing more. In it's final version, the app blocker now lets you create block sets, choose what time and days a set is active, has settings for various things, a polished UI, etc.

But if I started with that finished, polished app in mind, I would probably end up doing a lot of work in the first few days, then feel despair at how much is still left, and give up and move on to the next shiny project. By limiting scope, getting something working, and refining and adding nice to haves later, I was able to continuously make progress, which kept up my motivation to continue the project.

Additionally, refining doesn't spark as much joy. Seeing a new feature, looking at actual progress feels good and so keeps you coming back. But if you get stuck up on making it perfect, progress stalls, it can get very unsatisfactory and it leads to abandonment

So start small, start basic, get it working, then make it good.

4. Make progress every day, however small, non negotiable

Force yourself to make some small progress everyday. Mandatory. There were many days I did a very little work, like fixing colors for a few buttons, or beautifying the display of one column. But I always ensured I did something every day.

This keeps you in touch with your codebase, keeps it fresh in your mind, and makes abandonment of the project less likely. (if you've ever opened a project after ages, you know that it takes some time to refamiliarize yourself with its structure and flows, and that itself can give you enough reason to postpone working on it, eventually abandoning it).

By letting yourself do small amounts on days you don't feel too good, you're also more likely to do the work, because there's no pressure before starting that something significant has to be achieved.

I often find that just by starting on many days, I get caught up in the flow and do a lot more than I intended to.

If you miss a day or two, just focus on getting back on track ASAP. Eventually these small wins will add up to a finished, working app.
